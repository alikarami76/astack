# How large firms run online experimentation: findings for a marketplace analyst agent

Method note: sources were located by web search and several were read directly (Johari et al. abstract, Airbnb interference arXiv paper, LinkedIn budget-split blog, Spotify sequential-testing post). Some blog pages returned 403 or 504, so those claims rest on search-result summaries; they are marked "(snippet)". Items marked "(background knowledge)" are standard practice I did not re-verify in this session. Treat exact numbers as indicative.

## 1. Platforms and their distinctive methods

**Microsoft ExP.** The team led by Ron Kohavi produced the reference book and the SRM taxonomy. Its KDD'19 paper classifies sample-ratio mismatch (SRM) causes by stage: assignment (bad bucketing, carry-over), execution (redirects, variant changes engagement), log processing (bad joins), analysis (segmenting on post-treatment conditions). ExP built self-serve SRM diagnosis into the platform. https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/diagnosing-sample-ratio-mismatch-in-online-controlled-experiments-a-taxonomy-and-rules-of-thumb-for-practitioners/ and https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/diagnosing-sample-ratio-mismatch-in-a-b-testing/. Also the origin of CUPED (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2433396.2433413), the delta-method guide for ratio metrics (https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06336), "Seven Rules of Thumb" (https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2014%20experimentersRulesOfThumb.pdf) and "A/B Testing Intuition Busters" (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3534678.3539160). Book: "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments" (Kohavi, Tang, Xu), which covers OEC, guardrails, Twyman's law, triggering: https://experimentguide.com/ and https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/twymans-law-and-experimentation-trustworthiness/886425EC1B92BD23A0DC5E6817785190.

**Booking.com.** Democratised, decentralised experimentation: any team can run tests, backed by a central repository of successes and failures, a generic extensible code library, monitoring of data pipelines and safeguards; reported about 1,000 concurrent experiments. https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08217. Its ML paper stresses an iterative, hypothesis-driven process across about 150 models, and that offline model metrics often do not translate into business gains, so online tests decide: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/150-Successful-Machine-Learning-Models:-6-Lessons-Bernardi-Mavridis/07d2ee90b475d2c0d1ed91f6485e86784f61189f.

**Netflix.** Two-stage testing for ranking algorithms: fast interleaving to prune candidates in days, then a classic A/B test for long-term member behaviour (https://netflixtechblog.com/using-interleaving-in-online-experiments-to-accelerate-algorithm-innovation-at-netflix-a04ee392ec55). "Quasimodo" automates quasi-experiments when randomisation is impossible (https://netflixtechblog.com/quasi-experimentation-at-netflix-566b57d2e362). Consumer tests run at least a month to see retention (snippet: https://netflixtechblog.com/a-b-testing-and-beyond-improving-the-netflix-streaming-experience-with-experimentation-and-data-5b0ae9295bdf). Heterogeneous treatment effects: https://netflixtechblog.medium.com/heterogeneous-treatment-effects-at-netflix-da5c3dd58833. Surrogate index tested on 200 A/B tests: decisions from 14 days of surrogates agreed with 63-day direct measurement about 95% of the time (snippet): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11922. Platform paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.03878.

**Airbnb.** ERF (Experiment Reporting Framework), started 2014, defines metrics in a shared config language and computes them per experiment ("Scaling Airbnb's Experimentation Platform": https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/https-medium-com-jonathan-parks-scaling-erf-23fd17c91166). Guardrails framework with three parts: a Power Guardrail (enough exposure so impact guardrail is meaningful), an Impact Guardrail, and a Stat-Sig Negative Guardrail that escalates any significant harm however small: https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/designing-experimentation-guardrails-ed6a976ec669. Interference work is covered in section 3. Ron Kohavi was also at Airbnb.

**Uber (XP).** Over 1,000 concurrent experiments. Fixed-horizon tests, SPRT and mSPRT sequential monitoring (with FDR handling for many metrics), causal-inference methods (synthetic control, diff-in-diff), and switchback / time-split designs for marketplace changes. https://eng.uber.com/xp/ , https://www.uber.com/blog/supercharging-a-b-testing-at-uber/ , https://www.uber.com/blog/experimentation-platform/ .

**LinkedIn.** T-REX (Targeting, Ramping, EXperimentation) does Bernoulli randomisation with t-test readout and ignores interaction between units by default. For social-graph interference LinkedIn built ego-cluster randomisation (ego plus alters as a cluster), v2 uses one-degree label propagation for about 5x more clusters at similar quality: https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08755 and https://arxiv.org/html/2308.05945v3. For ads marketplace it uses budget-split testing (section 3): https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/infrastructure/budget-split-testing.

**Spotify (Confidence).** Uses group sequential tests (GST) because its data arrive in batches; also offers always-valid inference. Spotify's own comparison: GST has higher power when you can estimate final sample size in advance; always-valid inference (mSPRT and generalised AVI) is safer when you cannot, at a modest power cost. https://engineering.atspotify.com/2023/03/choosing-sequential-testing-framework-comparisons-and-discussions and https://confidence.spotify.com/docs/experiments/stats/sequential-tests. Trigger analysis to reduce dilution: https://confidence.spotify.com/blog/trigger-analysis.

**Meta.** PlanOut separates experiment design from application code (https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/designing-and-deploying-online-field-experiments/). Asymmetric holdouts: keep a small group back from launched positive changes to measure long-term impact, with more users in the test (held-back) arm than usual (snippet: https://medium.com/@AnalyticsAtMeta/the-power-of-asymmetric-experiments-meta-8a8030d68c31). Surrogate index theory: Athey, Chetty, Imbens, Kang (https://d2cml-ai.github.io/mgtecon634_py/notebooks/9_surrogate_py.html summarises it).

**Google.** Overlapping experiment infrastructure: orthogonal "layers" (each layer owns a set of parameters), experiments in different layers overlap on the same traffic, "domains" partition traffic, and layers can nest. Lets thousands of experiments run at once; interactions are handled by putting parameters that interact in the same layer. https://research.google/blog/overlapping-experiment-infrastructure-more-better-faster-experimentation/ and https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1835804.1835810. Google also produced budget-split-style design work for ads (section 3).

**Amazon (Weblab).** Kohavi led Weblab there (https://www.amazon.com/Trustworthy-Online-Controlled-Experiments-Practical-ebook/dp/B0845Y3DJV author bio); public detail on internals is thin. Amazon Science published the "double-randomised" / multiple-randomisation design for buyer-seller interference: https://assets.amazon.science/c1/94/0d6431bf46f7978295d245dd6e06/double-randomized-online-experiments.pdf and the formal treatment https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.13495 (JRSS-B 2026 version: https://academic.oup.com/jrsssb/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/jrsssb/qkaf073/8422435).

**DoorDash.** Switchback tests with independently randomised time-window by region units (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/switchback-tests-and-randomized-experimentation-under-network-effects-at-doordash/), cluster-robust standard errors to avoid false positives from correlated windows (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/cluster-robust-standard-error-in-switchback-experiments/), rigour checks (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/experiment-rigor-for-switchback-experiment-analysis/), the trade-off among network effects, learning effects and power (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/balancing-network-effects-learning-effects-and-power-in-experiments/), CUPAC (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/improving-experimental-power-through-control-using-predictions-as-covariate-cupac/), Dash-AB stats engine (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/meet-dash-ab-the-statistics-engine-of-experimentation-at-doordash/). Variance reduction generally cut required sample size by about 10-20% (snippet).

**Lyft.** Time-split tests for pricing, ETAs, routing, but naive versions suffer from storms, events and outages (https://eng.lyft.com/challenges-in-experimentation-be9ab98a7ef4). Marketplace marginal values (shadow prices) correct interference bias in user-split tests (https://eng.lyft.com/using-marketplace-marginal-values-to-address-interference-bias-a11aff6e670f; paper https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02274). Overview: https://eng.lyft.com/experimentation-in-a-ridesharing-marketplace-36007a8a31f2.

**Etsy.** Handles peeking with UI changes plus a flexible p-value stopping point inspired by sequential testing (https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/how-etsy-handles-peeking-in-a-b-testing); predicted control variates to shorten experiments (https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/reducing-experiment-duration-with-predicted-control-variates); double-bucketing edge case when splits are 5/95 (https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/double-bucketing-in-ab-testing).

**Zalando.** Octopus platform, two-sided t-test at 5%, standardised randomisation and analysis, analysis methods peer-reviewed by applied scientists from other teams, leading to non-inferiority tests. https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2021/01/experimentation-platform-part1.html.

**Commercial (brief).** Optimizely Stats Engine (2015): mSPRT always-valid p-values plus tiered Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control (https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922, https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410283967245-False-discovery-rate-control); newer product offers fixed-horizon, sequential and Bayesian engines plus CUPED. Statsig and Eppo: CUPED, sequential testing, Bayesian and frequentist, Statsig adds switchback and Benjamini-Hochberg (https://www.statsig.com/updates/update/benjamini-hochberg). GrowthBook: Bayesian default, frequentist with sequential and CUPED as options. Comparison pages (vendor-written, biased): https://www.statsig.com/vs/eppo , https://www.growthbook.io/compare/growthbook-vs-statsig.

## 2. Core methods and pitfalls as practiced

**SRM check.** Chi-square goodness-of-fit test of observed vs designed allocation. Microsoft practice is to use a very strict threshold (p < 0.001, background knowledge) and to treat a failed check as "results invalid, do not read" until the cause is found. Taxonomy of causes above (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/diagnosing-sample-ratio-mismatch-in-a-b-testing/). Segmenting or filtering on anything affected by treatment causes SRM.

**CUPED / CUPAC.** CUPED subtracts a pre-experiment covariate (often the same metric before the test) times theta = cov(Y,X)/var(X); the estimator stays unbiased and variance shrinks by roughly (1 - rho^2) (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2433396.2433413). CUPAC replaces the covariate with an ML prediction of the outcome built from pre-period data (DoorDash, https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/improving-experimental-power-through-control-using-predictions-as-covariate-cupac/; Etsy variant above). Covariates must be pre-treatment, and the fitted model must not use treatment-period data.

**Ratio metrics and delta method.** When the randomisation unit (user) differs from the metric denominator (orders per session, conversion per view), a naive t-test on row-level ratios ignores intra-user correlation and understates variance. Use the delta method for the variance of the ratio of means at the randomisation-unit level (https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06336; problem discussion https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.03553). Bootstrap over units is the simple alternative.

**Sequential testing and peeking.** Repeatedly checking a fixed-horizon p-value inflates false positives well beyond 5% (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.04922, KDD'17 version http://library.usc.edu.ph/ACM/KKD%202017/pdfs/p1517.pdf). Remedies: always-valid inference via mSPRT (Optimizely, Uber), group sequential tests with pre-declared looks (Spotify), or Etsy's mixed UI-plus-statistics approach. Choice guide from Spotify's post above. Uber uses mSPRT for continuous metric monitoring (https://www.uber.com/blog/supercharging-a-b-testing-at-uber/).

**Bayesian vs frequentist.** Big platforms mostly report frequentist results with sequential corrections; commercial vendors offer both (Optimizely, Statsig, GrowthBook links above). A Bayesian readout does not remove peeking issues by itself if you stop on a posterior threshold without calibration (background knowledge). Pre-registered decision rules matter more than the paradigm.

**Multiple testing and FDR.** With many metrics and variants, correct significance. Optimizely uses tiered Benjamini-Hochberg; Statsig offers BH; Spotify discusses Bonferroni-family corrections. Common practice: one primary metric (OEC), designate guardrails, correct or clearly label secondary metrics. https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410283967245-False-discovery-rate-control , https://ron-berman.com/papers/fdr.pdf (empirical false discovery in A/B testing).

**OEC and guardrails.** OEC (overall evaluation criterion) is a single metric or a weighted combination that predicts long-term value, agreed before the test (Kohavi book above). Guardrails: two types per Microsoft usage, metrics you must not hurt (revenue, cancellations, latency) and invariants that should not move by design (allocation ratio, cache hits). Airbnb's guardrail design adds a power requirement so that "no significant harm" is meaningful: https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/designing-experimentation-guardrails-ed6a976ec669.

**Novelty and primacy effects.** Initial lift from novelty fades; initial dip from primacy recovers. Detect by plotting daily treatment effect over time and splitting new vs returning users (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.05310 lists it as a known challenge; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3534678.3539160). Run at least one to two full weekly cycles, longer for habits.

**Long-term holdouts and surrogates.** Keep a small holdout after launch (Meta's asymmetric design), or predict long-term outcomes from short-term surrogates (Athey et al.; Netflix validation: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11922). Surrogates fail when the treatment affects the long-term outcome through paths the surrogates do not capture.

**Heterogeneous treatment effects.** Netflix uses HTE analysis to understand who is affected and to annualise impact (https://netflixtechblog.medium.com/heterogeneous-treatment-effects-at-netflix-da5c3dd58833). Pitfall: slicing many segments is multiple testing; pre-specify segments (for a marketplace: seller size, category, new vs established seller, price tier).

**Twyman's law.** Any figure that looks too good or too interesting is usually wrong; check instrumentation, SRM and outliers before celebrating (book chapter link above).

**A/A tests.** Run identical variants to validate randomisation, variance estimates and that p-values are uniform; used by Microsoft and others (Kohavi book; background knowledge). Cheap offline version: many random splits of past data (see section 4).

**Triggering and dilution.** Analyse only units that could have been affected (they saw the feature); untriggered units have zero effect and only add noise. Requires that the trigger is defined the same way in both arms (counterfactual or "shadow" logging), and results are then diluted back to the whole population for the launch decision. https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/triggering-ab-tests-ronny-kohavi , https://confidence.spotify.com/blog/trigger-analysis , https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/patterns-of-trustworthy-experimentation-post-experiment-stage/.

**Clustered randomisation.** Randomise groups (geo, cluster, time window) instead of individuals when units interfere; analysis must use cluster-robust errors and the effective sample size is the number of clusters, not units (DoorDash cluster-robust post above; Airbnb below).

## 3. Marketplace and two-sided interference

**The core problem.** Standard user-level A/B tests assume one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome (SUTVA). In a marketplace, treated and control units compete for the same demand, supply or budget. Example from Airbnb: forcing treated hosts to lower prices raises their bookings and lowers control hosts' bookings, so the treated-minus-control difference overstates the true effect of rolling out to everyone; naive difference in means "can lead to estimates that are inflated by a factor of 100%" in the simulated case. https://arxiv.org/html/2004.12162v1 (read directly) and the published cluster-randomisation version https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.01157.

**Which side to randomise depends on which side is scarce.** Johari, Li, Liskovich, Weintraub: in demand-constrained markets, randomising customers is nearly unbiased and randomising listings is biased; in supply-constrained markets the reverse holds; in balanced markets both are biased. Their two-sided randomisation (randomise both sides at once) is unbiased at both extremes and reasonably accurate in between. https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05670 (read directly). Implication for a marketplace: a seller-panel change or seller-side price test in a supply-rich category behaves differently from a flash-sale where stock is limited.

**Multiple randomisation / double randomisation designs.** Buyers and sellers are randomised independently, outcome is measured at the buyer-seller pair level, giving identifiable direct and spillover effects (Amazon Science and Bajari et al.; Liu-Shaikh et al. randomisation inference: https://home.uchicago.edu/amshaikh/webfiles/doublerand.pdf; regression adjustment: https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19480). Cost: needs enough buyers and sellers, and pair-level design.

**Cluster randomisation with a bias-variance trade-off.** Airbnb simulation: graph-cluster randomisation with blocking cut bias by up to 62% but raised variance by 75%; the authors advise using it only when you have strong reason to expect large interference, because the higher minimum detectable effect may make tests unusable (https://arxiv.org/html/2004.12162v1). A live Airbnb pricing meta-experiment confirmed interference bias exists and that cluster randomisation reduces it: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.01157 and https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3583836. Douyin case study on correcting interference: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02542.

**Switchback / time-split.** Whole market (or whole region) gets treatment or control in alternating time windows, so no two units in the same window interfere. Used at DoorDash, Lyft, Uber (links section 1). Requirements: randomise the window assignment (do not alternate deterministically), pick windows longer than the interference and carry-over duration, randomise across regions independently, use cluster-robust standard errors with the window as cluster, and expect low power because the effective N is the number of windows. DoorDash discusses the trade-off between network effects (want long windows or big units to avoid bias) and power/learning effects (https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/balancing-network-effects-learning-effects-and-power-in-experiments/). Lyft warns that storms, big events and outages contaminate windows (https://eng.lyft.com/challenges-in-experimentation-be9ab98a7ef4). Design theory: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06768 and multi-unit switchbacks https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.12654. Switchbacks are a poor fit when effects carry over (price changes that shift future demand, stockpiling) or when the treatment is visible to sellers across windows (sellers learn and react).

**Geo randomisation.** Randomise whole regions; suits changes that affect a whole local market but yields few units, so pair with synthetic control or diff-in-diff (Uber lists synthetic control and diff-in-diff: https://eng.uber.com/xp/). In a marketplace, cities or warehouse regions are candidate units, but delivery, national sellers and national campaigns leak across regions (background knowledge).

**Budget-split.** For ad-like or budget-constrained promotions: split users into two groups and split each campaign/seller budget into two identical sub-campaigns, one per group, so the two arms cannot cannibalise each other's budget. Unbiased for any market where buyers have budgets; LinkedIn measured a 30-70% difference from the naive test and up to 10x sensitivity (weeks reduced to 1-3 days). https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08724 and https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/infrastructure/budget-split-testing. Analogy in a marketplace: promotion budgets, daily-deal inventory caps or seller-funded discount pools can be split in proportion to arm size.

**Shadow-price / marginal-value correction.** Instead of comparing raw outcomes, compare the marginal value each arm consumes or contributes from the scarce resource; in simulation this yields essentially unbiased estimates and in Lyft's data a user-split test overstated magnitudes by about 45% on average in resource-constrained experiments (snippet). https://eng.lyft.com/using-marketplace-marginal-values-to-address-interference-bias-a11aff6e670f and https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02274.

**Pricing experiments.** Airbnb's Smart Pricing work identifies lead-day bias: because outcomes for future stay dates arrive later and pricing outputs drift, a short test can point the opposite way from the long-term result; they propose an estimator to remove the bias. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3583780.3615502 (paper: https://alexdeng.github.io/public/files/Smart_Pricing_CIKM.pdf; full text not parsed here). General lessons: price tests have strong substitution between treated and control items in the same category (cannibalisation), competitors and sellers reprice in response, and demand can be pulled forward. Randomise at the level where substitution occurs (product group, category-region) rather than the buyer.

**Promotion experiments.** Same issues: pull-forward (demand shifts from days after the promotion to the promotion), cannibalisation across products and sellers, inventory limits, and seller reaction over time. Measure post-period, not just the promo window; use holdouts of products or sellers at the level of substitution; read total category or seller outcome, not only the treated items (background knowledge; supported by the interference sources above).

**Seller-side vs buyer-side.** Randomising buyers to see different prices or badges makes sellers see mixed demand and can mask seller response; randomising sellers to a feature makes treated and control sellers compete for the same buyers. Choose based on which side is constrained in that category (Johari et al.), consider two-sided randomisation, and report both direct and total effects where possible.

**Practical reading rule.** Naive user-level tests in a marketplace usually overstate the effect of a full rollout when treatment steals from control; interference bias direction depends on whether the treatment is a substitute or complement. Interference-corrected designs cost power (Airbnb variance +75%), so the honest output is often "the naive estimate is an upper bound, here is a bound and a design that could tighten it".

## 4. What a small team with SQL and Python can copy

1. **Write the plan before the test.** One primary metric (OEC), 2-4 guardrails, minimum detectable effect, unit of randomisation, run length, decision rule. (Kohavi book; Airbnb guardrail post.)
2. **Deterministic hash assignment.** Hash (experiment_id + unit_id) to buckets in SQL/Python; store assignments in a table so they can be joined later. Never assign through mutable attributes. (Booking, Etsy, Google layers idea for salting per experiment.)
3. **Always run the SRM chi-square first** on assignment counts, and again on triggered counts. Stop reading if p < 0.001. `scipy.stats.chisquare`.
4. **Power and duration calculation up front** using historic variance of the metric at the randomisation-unit level; run whole weeks (weekday effects; marketplaces also have calendar and campaign effects such as sales events).
5. **Analyse at the randomisation-unit level.** Aggregate to seller or buyer before testing; use delta method or bootstrap for ratios (https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06336). Watch heavy tails: use capped/winsorised metrics or bootstrap, and report both.
6. **CUPED in plain SQL and numpy.** Compute the same metric per unit in the pre-period (2-4 weeks), theta = cov/var, adjust. Expect the largest gains on high-repeat metrics such as seller sales and buyer orders. Verify by checking that the adjusted variance is lower and the mean effect similar.
7. **Offline A/A simulation.** Repeatedly split historical units randomly, apply your test pipeline, and check that the false positive rate is about 5% and p-values are uniform. Also a placebo test: compute the metric difference in the pre-period between arms.
8. **Avoid peeking.** Either fix the horizon, or pre-declare a small number of looks with a group sequential boundary (for example O'Brien-Fleming style, background knowledge), or use an mSPRT always-valid p-value (short formula in https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922). Show dashboards without p-values until the end (Etsy's UI approach).
9. **Multiple metrics.** Apply Benjamini-Hochberg to secondary metrics (`statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests`), label exploratory segments as hypotheses, pre-register HTE segments.
10. **Triggered analysis with dilution.** Log exposure (who actually saw the deal or panel feature) in both arms; analyse exposed units, then dilute for the launch estimate.
11. **Interference-aware design that fits SQL.** (a) Randomise at the substitution unit (product group, category, seller) rather than at the buyer for price and promo tests; (b) switchback by day or city-day for platform-wide parameters, randomised window order, cluster-robust or block bootstrap by window, excluding storm/holiday windows; (c) budget-split for promotion pools; (d) staggered rollout with diff-in-diff or synthetic control by region when randomisation is impossible (Uber, Netflix quasi-experimentation links above); (e) report both the naive and the interference-robust estimate and flag the gap.
12. **Long-term view.** Keep a 1-5% holdout after launch, and re-read at 4 and 12 weeks; check novelty by plotting daily effects and new vs returning splits.
13. **Sanity habits.** Twyman check on big wins, change log of experiments, a repository of past tests with outcomes (Booking practice) so priors improve.
